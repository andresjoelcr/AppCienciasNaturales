================================================================================
3. Study Cases
================================================================================

Two complementary case studies were designed to evaluate the proposed mobile
educational application from both empirical and technical perspectives. The first
case study involved a real-world implementation with secondary school students,
collecting usability, satisfaction, and pedagogical effectiveness data through
structured instruments. The second case study conducted a systematic technical
validation of the multi-model AI architecture, evaluating the performance,
reliability, and pedagogical quality of each AI subsystem under realistic
operational conditions. Together, these studies provide a triangulated evaluation
framework that addresses both the learner-facing experience and the engineering
soundness of the proposed system.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1. Real-World Implementation Study: User-Centered Evaluation of a Mobile-
     Assisted Natural Sciences Learning Environment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1.1. Study Design and Participants

A case study was conducted in a controlled real-world educational environment to
evaluate the behavior, functionality, usability, and learning impact of the
proposed mobile application integrated into a secondary school Natural Sciences
curriculum. The study followed a pre-experimental one-group pretest–posttest
design (Campbell & Stanley, 1963), considered appropriate for exploratory
technology-enhanced learning research in which random assignment is not feasible
due to institutional constraints (Shadish et al., 2002).

The study included 45 eighth-grade students from a public educational institution
in Carchi province, Ecuador. Participants ranged in age from 12 to 14 years
(M = 13.2, SD = 0.8), with a balanced gender distribution (51% female, 49% male).
Convenience sampling was employed, as the group constituted an intact classroom
cohort scheduled to cover the cellular biology unit during the intervention
period. Participation was voluntary; informed assent was obtained from all
students and informed consent from their legal guardians, in accordance with the
ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Anonymization procedures were
applied to all collected data throughout the study. Prior to the intervention, a
demographic screening revealed that 78% of participants reported prior experience
with educational mobile applications, whereas only 22% had previously interacted
with AI-powered chatbot systems, reflecting a realistic profile of digital
familiarity typical of the target population.

3.1.2. Procedure

The study was conducted over a four-week period and comprised three sequential
phases:

Phase I — System Familiarization (Week 1). During the first week, participants
received a structured onboarding session (90 minutes) facilitated by the research
team and the classroom teacher. The session introduced the application's five
functional modules: (i) the Interactive Didactic Guide, (ii) the Educational
Chatbot, (iii) the Smart Image Scanner, (iv) the Augmented Reality viewer, and
(v) the Scientific Glossary. Students were provided guided practice tasks for each
module to ensure baseline operational competence before independent use. A
pre-test instrument assessing prior knowledge of cellular biology was administered
at the end of this phase.

Phase II — Classroom Integration (Weeks 2–4). During the three-week integration
period, students used the application as a complementary learning tool alongside
regular in-person instruction. The classroom teacher incorporated the application
into the Natural Sciences curriculum unit on cellular structure and function,
assigning weekly tasks that required interaction with the chatbot, completion of
AI-generated quizzes for self-assessment, and use of the image scanner to identify
biological specimens encountered in laboratory activities and in the school's
natural environment. No restriction was placed on the frequency or modality of
use outside class hours, allowing naturalistic engagement patterns to emerge.
Interaction logs were automatically recorded in Firebase Firestore throughout this
phase, capturing timestamped events for each AI component.

Phase III — Data Collection and Post-Assessment (End of Week 4). At the
conclusion of the intervention period, a post-test on cellular biology knowledge
was administered using a parallel form to the pre-test instrument, developed by
two subject-matter experts with equivalent content coverage and cognitive demand.
Students then completed a structured questionnaire evaluating their perceptions of
the system across three dimensions: usability, satisfaction and usefulness, and
accessibility and interaction. Open-ended items invited qualitative feedback on
positive aspects and areas for improvement. The knowledge instruments and
perception questionnaires were administered during regular class time under the
supervision of the research team.

3.1.3. Pre-test and Post-test Knowledge Instruments

Knowledge gain was measured using a 20-item multiple-choice test on cellular
biology (structure, organelle function, cell cycle, comparison of animal and plant
cells), aligned with the national curriculum framework for eighth grade in Ecuador
(Ministerio de Educación del Ecuador, 2023). The two parallel forms were reviewed
by a panel of two experienced Natural Sciences teachers for content validity and
clarity, achieving consensus on all items. Internal consistency was estimated
using Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), yielding KR-20 = 0.79 for Form A
(pre-test) and KR-20 = 0.81 for Form B (post-test), indicating acceptable
reliability for formative assessment purposes (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994).
Effect size was quantified using Cohen's d (Cohen, 1988).

3.1.4. Perception Questionnaire

Student perceptions of the system were assessed using a structured questionnaire
developed ad hoc for this study (Table 1), informed by established instruments
in the human–computer interaction and educational technology literature, including
the System Usability Scale (SUS; Brooke, 1996) and the Technology Acceptance
Model (TAM; Davis, 1989). The questionnaire comprised ten Likert-type items
(1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree), distributed across three dimensions,
and two open-ended questions soliciting qualitative feedback. Additionally, the
full 10-item SUS questionnaire was administered to obtain a standardized usability
score (Bangor et al., 2009).

3.1.5. Statistical Analysis Plan

Quantitative perception data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (mean,
standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and frequency distributions by response
category). Given the ordinal nature of Likert-type items and the absence of
normality assumptions for small samples, non-parametric tests were employed for
group comparisons: the Mann–Whitney U test for gender comparisons (male vs.
female), and the Kruskal–Wallis H test with post-hoc Dunn–Bonferroni corrections
for multi-group comparisons by AI knowledge level and age range (Field, 2018).
Effect sizes for Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were reported as r and
η²_H, respectively (Cohen, 1988; Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014). Pre-post knowledge
differences were assessed using a paired-samples t-test, with Cohen's d as the
effect size estimator. Pearson's r was used to examine bivariate correlations
between gamification metrics and quiz performance, given the continuous nature of
both variables. Qualitative responses to open-ended items were analyzed through
thematic categorization (Braun & Clarke, 2006), with two independent raters
coding the data; inter-rater reliability was estimated using Cohen's κ. All
statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.11 (SciPy 1.11, statsmodels
0.14) and R 4.3.1. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

3.2. Technical Performance Evaluation of the Multi-Model AI Architecture
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.2.1. Rationale and Objectives

A systematic technical evaluation was designed to assess the operational
performance, reliability, and pedagogical quality of the three AI subsystems
integrated into the application: (i) the educational chatbot based on LLaMA 3.3
70B, (ii) the adaptive quiz generation module, and (iii) the multi-stage image
recognition pipeline. This evaluation addressed two complementary objectives:
first, to quantify system-level indicators of availability, latency, and
fault-tolerance under real usage conditions; second, to assess the pedagogical
validity of AI-generated outputs through expert evaluation, providing evidence
beyond purely technical metrics. This dual-focus evaluation design addresses a
recognized gap in the educational AI literature, in which system performance is
frequently reported in isolation from pedagogical quality (Pérez et al., 2025;
Okonkwo & Ade-Ibijola, 2021).

3.2.2. Interaction Logging and Technical Metrics Collection

Interaction data were automatically captured throughout the four-week study
period via a structured logging layer implemented in Firebase Firestore. Each
AI-mediated event was recorded with the following fields: component identifier
(chatbot, quiz generator, image scanner), timestamp, processing stage (for the
multi-stage image pipeline), response latency in milliseconds, outcome status
(success/failure/fallback-activated), token counts for Groq API interactions
(input tokens, output tokens), and anonymized session identifier. This
event-level logging enabled computation of the following technical performance
indicators for each component:

  — Mean, median, and standard deviation of response latency
  — 95th and 99th percentile latency (P95, P99), as indicators of tail latency
    relevant to user experience under load
  — Total requests, successes, failures, and fallback activations
  — Component-level success rate (%) = successful responses / total requests
  — System-level availability (%) = uptime intervals / total observation intervals
  — Token consumption per interaction (input and output) for Groq API components
  — Fallback distribution across pipeline stages (image scanner)

For the image recognition pipeline specifically, the graceful degradation
architecture operates across three sequential stages: (i) Gemini 1.5 Flash as
the primary visual language model, (ii) TensorFlow Lite MobileNet V2 as the
on-device fallback triggered upon Gemini failure or timeout, and (iii) Groq
LLaMA 3.3 70B as a tertiary description-enrichment stage. Technical metrics
were computed independently for each stage, as well as aggregated for the
complete pipeline, to allow isolation of the contribution of each fallback layer
to overall system availability.

3.2.3. Expert Evaluation of AI-Generated Content

Given that technical availability and latency metrics do not capture the
pedagogical quality of AI-generated outputs, an expert evaluation protocol was
implemented for each AI subsystem. Two certified Natural Sciences teachers with
a minimum of five years of secondary school teaching experience served as
independent evaluators. Both evaluators were blinded to the study hypotheses
and were compensated according to institutional guidelines.

Chatbot Response Quality. A stratified random sample of 100 chatbot interactions
was selected from the interaction logs (proportional allocation across the five
most frequent query types identified through content analysis). Each interaction
was presented to both evaluators on a standardized rubric comprising four
pedagogical quality criteria, rated on a 1–5 scale:

  (1) Scientific accuracy: degree to which the response is factually correct
      and consistent with the curricular content.
  (2) Clarity and comprehensibility: degree to which the response is expressed
      clearly and is appropriate for the reading level of eighth-grade students.
  (3) Adequacy to educational level: degree to which the vocabulary, examples,
      and conceptual depth match the cognitive demands of the target learners.
  (4) Pedagogical relevance: degree to which the response supports achievement
      of the cellular biology learning objectives.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen's weighted κ (Fleiss & Cohen,
1973). Cases of evaluator disagreement exceeding two scale points were
adjudicated through consensus discussion.

Quiz Item Pedagogical Validity. A stratified random sample of 150 AI-generated
quiz items (50 per difficulty level: easy, medium, hard) was selected for expert
review. Items were evaluated against six pedagogical validity criteria:

  (1) Clarity and comprehensibility of the stem
  (2) Validity of the correct answer
  (3) Plausibility of the distractors
  (4) Appropriateness of the assigned difficulty level
  (5) Alignment with the cellular biology curriculum
  (6) Educational utility of the explanatory feedback

Additionally, item-level psychometric properties were computed for a sample of
200 items based on student response data: the item difficulty index (p, proportion
of students answering correctly) and the item discrimination index (D, point-
biserial correlation between item response and total quiz score). Optimal item
difficulty was defined as p ∈ [0.30, 0.80] (Lord, 1952), and acceptable
discrimination as D ≥ 0.20 (Ebel & Frisbie, 1991).

Image Recognition Accuracy Assessment. To obtain ground-truth accuracy estimates
independent of the automated logging, a random sample of 200 images processed
during the study was manually labeled by two independent raters. Labeling accuracy
was defined as the proportion of cases in which the AI-generated identification
matched the expert-assigned label. Inter-rater reliability for the ground-truth
labeling was estimated using Cohen's κ. Accuracy was computed separately for
each pipeline stage and for each biological object category (cells and organelles,
plants, animals, microorganisms, and laboratory equipment).

Educational Description Quality. For the image scanner's generated textual
descriptions, a subsample of 100 outputs was evaluated by both experts using a
four-criterion rubric (accuracy of information, educational relevance, clarity
and comprehensibility, and quality of the scientific curiosity datum), each
rated on a 1–5 scale.

3.2.4. Domain Restriction Effectiveness Evaluation

A targeted evaluation of the chatbot's domain restriction mechanism was conducted
to quantify the system's ability to maintain topical focus on Natural Sciences
and reject out-of-domain queries. From the complete interaction log, all
interactions flagged as potential out-of-domain queries were identified through
keyword-based heuristic screening, followed by manual expert review of the
resulting subset. Each flagged query was classified into one of four categories:
(i) unrelated academic topics (e.g., mathematics, history), (ii) personal or
entertainment queries, (iii) requests for inappropriate content, and (iv) boundary
or ambiguous cases with partial thematic overlap. For each category, the
proportion of correctly rejected queries (redirected appropriately to Natural
Sciences content) was computed, yielding a domain restriction effectiveness rate.

3.2.5. Token Consumption and Scalability Analysis

Given the pedagogical and economic implications of large-scale deployment, token
consumption was monitored for all Groq API interactions. For the educational
chatbot, the dominant source of token consumption is the curricular context
injection strategy, whereby the complete content of the didactic guide (~4,000
tokens) is prepended to the system prompt in each interaction. This strategy
deliberately anchors LLM responses in verified educational material but entails
a non-trivial per-interaction token cost. Token counts for input and output were
computed per component (chatbot, quiz generator, image description enrichment)
and reported as descriptive statistics, enabling cost–benefit analysis of the
context injection approach relative to alternatives such as retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) or periodic fine-tuning.
